Lesson 2

Testing the model in Ho Chi Minh City

Lesson 1 predicts that land prices and population density fall with distance from the centre. This lesson measures both gradients for Ho Chi Minh City, locates the edge of the city with population data rather than with its administrative boundary, compares the density gradient with 192 cities, and identifies where the city departs from the model.

Data: the Ho Chi Minh City land price table applied from 1 January 2026; GHS-POP 2020 and WorldPop 2020 population grids; the GHSL Urban Centre Database, 1975–2030. Numbers in the text are produced by kich_ban/l2_tphcm.mjs; quotations are checked against the source PDFs by kich_ban/trich_dan.mjs.

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to:

  1. estimate a price or density gradient with a semi-logarithmic regression, and interpret its coefficient;
  2. explain why an administratively set land price is weak evidence about a market bid-rent curve;
  3. assess how choices of measurement change an estimated gradient;
  4. distinguish the administrative boundary of a city from its urban extent measured with gridded population data, and relate both to the edge of the city in the model;
  5. compare Ho Chi Minh City with other cities, and identify the differences in measurement that limit the comparison.

2.1Measuring a gradient

The model predicts the direction of the gradients but not their functional form. Empirical studies usually summarise a gradient with a semi-logarithmic regression, a form that goes back to Clark (1951):

\[ \ln y_i = a + b\,x_i + \varepsilon_i \]
(2.1)

Here \(y_i\) is the price or density of observation \(i\), \(x_i\) its distance in kilometres from the centre, and \(b\) the gradient. One kilometre farther out, \(y\) is multiplied by \(e^{b}\), a change of \(100\,(e^{b}-1)\) percent; for small values of \(b\) this is close to \(100\,b\) percent.

Log points. A coefficient of \(b = -0.08\) is a fall of 8 log points per km, or \(100(e^{-0.08}-1) = -7.7\) percent.

Three choices must be made before equation (2.1) can be estimated: the location of the centre, the variable that is measured, and the observations that enter the regression. This lesson places the centre at Ben Thanh station on Metro Line 1, in the historic core of the city, and discusses the other two choices in each section.

2.3Where does Ho Chi Minh City end?

Equation (1.5) closes the model of Lesson 1: the city ends at the distance \(\bar{x}\) where land rent falls to the rent that land earns in agriculture. The edge is an outcome of the land market. Nothing in the model makes it coincide with the boundary that the law draws around a city, and a gradient measured inside that boundary may include land beyond \(\bar{x}\) and leave out land within it.

The edge in land prices

The land price table also prices agricultural land, so the condition \(r(\bar{x}) = r_A\) can be examined from prices as well. The regulation attached to Resolution 87/2025 assigns each of the city’s wards, communes and special zone to one of four agricultural zones (Article 3), and prices agricultural land by zone and by position (Article 5). A plot is in position 1 if it lies within 200 m of a road named in the table, which makes position 1 comparable with residential prices, which are also set for plots on named roads. Table 2.2 gives the prices for annual crops, a category that includes rice.

Agricultural land prices vary little across the city: position 1 in zone I is priced at position 1 in zone IV. Figure 2.2 sets both prices along a section through the city, with the stretch that lies inside the 2020 urban centre shaded.

Figure 2.2 can also be drawn on the map itself. In Map 2.4 the prices of each ward stand above its place on the section line, on the same logarithmic scale, so that the reader can see which wards the section passes through and where it crosses the outline of the urban centre.

Nowhere in the city does the residential price fall to the price of agricultural land: the lowest ratio of the two, in Chau Duc commune, is . The price table therefore does not locate \(\bar{x}\) by itself; the population grid of Map 2.3 does so more directly.

Interpretation — not tested in this lessonTwo features of the table can keep the ratio above 1 even in rural communes. Residential prices are set only for plots on named roads, where some housing and services exist almost everywhere. And agricultural prices are administered, not market rents: twelve prices for the whole city cannot record how much a farmer would actually pay for land at a given place.

2.4Population density

Population density does not suffer from the problem of administered prices, and it can be measured on a regular grid. GHS-POP, produced by the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre, estimates the population of each 100 m cell. Here the cells are added up into a grid of 1 km cells within 40 km of Ben Thanh, and density is population divided by the area of each cell (Map 2.5, which also shows the outline of the 2020 urban centre from Map 2.3). In total, the circle contains people in 2020.

Why 300 and 1,500? The Degree of Urbanisation classifies grid cells with at least 300 persons per km² as potentially part of an urban cluster, and at least 1,500 as potentially part of an urban centre (Dijkstra et al., 2021).

2.6Where the model does not fit

Two features of Ho Chi Minh City are not described by the monocentric model of Lesson 1.

A flat centre

The model predicts that density is highest at the CBD and falls most steeply near it (Figure 1.1). In Figure 2.3 density changes little over the first 5 km. The literature reports the same pattern in other cities:

Interpretation — not tested in this lessonIn Ho Chi Minh City, offices, shops, public buildings and markets may occupy much of the land nearest Ben Thanh, leaving less of it for housing. The model has a single land use and cannot show this. A test would require the number of jobs in each cell, which this lesson does not use.

More than one centre

Beyond 20 km, density stops falling near Thu Dau Mot (Figure 2.3), and land prices in Thu Dau Mot, Ba Ria and Vung Tau are several times those of wards at the same distance from Ben Thanh (Table 2.1). Exercise 2.1Interpreting a coefficient

A regression of ln density on distance gives \(b = -0.120\).

  1. By what percentage does density change per kilometre?
  2. What is the ratio of density at 15 km to density at 5 km?
Show answer
(a) 100(e−0.12 − 1) = −11.3 percent per km. (b) e−0.12 × 10 = e−1.2 ≈ 0.30: density at 15 km is about 30 percent of density at 5 km.

Exercise 2.2Set prices and market prices

Suppose that market prices near Ben Thanh are several times the prices in the land price table, while near the edge of the city the two are similar. Would the gradient of market prices be steeper or flatter than the gradient in Figure 2.1? What data would you need to check?

Show answer
Steeper: the gap between market and table prices would be largest where prices are highest, near the centre. To check, you need market transaction prices of land, with the location of each transaction.

Exercise 2.3Where does the city end?

Use Map 2.3 and Figure 2.2.

  1. Name one place that is part of the city in the sense of the model but not in the administrative sense, and one place for which the reverse is true.
  2. Along the section, the urban centre extends much farther north of Ben Thanh than south. Which assumptions of Lesson 1 does this contradict? Give two possible reasons.
  3. In Figure 2.2, compare residential prices just inside and just outside the shaded band, at its southern end and at its northern end. Does the edge of the urban centre look like the point where \(r(\bar{x}) = r_A\)?
  4. Would a density gradient estimated only on cells inside the administrative boundary be steeper or flatter than one estimated on a circle around Ben Thanh? Explain.
  5. In step 6, why is the urban centre of 1990 much larger than the built-up land inside it? What does this imply if you compare the area of a city over time?
Show answer
(a) Bien Hoa, in Dong Nai province, lies inside the urban centre but outside the administrative boundary; the mangroves of Can Gio and the rural communes around Bau Bang lie inside the boundary but outside the urban centre. (b) The model assumes that commuting costs are the same in every direction and that the same share of land is available for housing at every distance. To the north, main roads lower commuting costs and industrial areas in the former Binh Duong province provide jobs outside the centre; to the south, rivers and mangroves reduce the land available for housing, \(\theta\) in equation (1.6). (c) In the south, prices fall sharply between the last ward inside the band and the first commune outside it; in the north they are already close to rural levels before the band ends. In neither place does the residential price come close to the agricultural price, so the price table does not show the condition of equation (1.5). (d) Probably steeper: the boundary adds sparsely populated rural land far from Ben Thanh and removes dense cells in Bien Hoa at a similar distance. Estimating both would check this. (e) The outline joins whole 1 km cells that pass a density threshold and fills the gaps between them, so it includes unbuilt land between dense villages and along rivers; and the population in each cell is itself estimated. Areas of such outlines in different years measure the rule as much as the city, so growth is better compared with a measure observed in each year, such as built-up land.
