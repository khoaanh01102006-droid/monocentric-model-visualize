Lesson 4

Ho Chi Minh City among its neighbours

Lessons 2 and 3 looked at one city. This lesson sets Ho Chi Minh City beside five other large cities of South-East Asia, drawn with the same definition of a city and at the same scale, so that differences in size, density and growth can be seen directly on the map. It then compares Ho Chi Minh City with Hanoi in more detail.

Data: the GHSL Urban Centre Database R2024A, the GHS-POP population grid of 2020 and the GHS-LAND land grid, published by the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre, and the World Settlement Footprint Evolution of the German Aerospace Center (DLR). Numbers in the text are produced by kich_ban/l4_do_thi_chau_a.py and kich_ban/l4_ha_noi.py.

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to:

  1. explain why cities can be compared only when they are measured with the same definition, and name the definition used here;
  2. read a density map drawn at the same scale for several cities, and compare their size, density and shape;
  3. relate the differences between cities to the comparative statics of Lesson 1, and say what such a comparison cannot show;
  4. compare Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City with built-up land observed from satellites and with density gradients, and explain why a gradient depends on how density is measured.

4.1One definition for every city

The administrative boundaries of cities are drawn for government, not for comparison: some enclose farmland and forest, others stop short of the built-up area. Lesson 2 therefore measured Ho Chi Minh City with the urban centre of the Global Human Settlement Layer, which is defined by population density alone:

Because the same rule is applied to the same population grid everywhere, the urban centre of Ho Chi Minh City can be set beside that of any other city. This lesson takes five: Hanoi, and four capitals of the region that are larger or similar in size.

Interpretation — not tested in this lessonBy the comparative statics of Lesson 1, a city with more people, higher incomes or cheaper commuting should cover more land. Across these six cities, the more populous urban centres are also the larger ones, but the cities differ at once in income, transport, geography and planning, so a comparison of six cases cannot separate these causes.

4.3Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City

Two of the six cities share a country. Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City have grown under the same land law and the same national policies, and in both the motorcycle is the main means of transport. Interpretation — consistent with the model, not a test of itIn the closed-city model, a larger population raises housing prices at every distance and pushes the edge of the city outward (Table 1.1); with dearer housing, households use less space and density rises. Ho Chi Minh City has the larger population and fits this description. The two cities also differ in incomes, in the kinds of jobs they offer and in geography — Hanoi sits in the densely settled Red River Delta — so the comparison agrees with the model without testing it.

Over 30 km, its gradient on all land is second only to that of Ho Chi Minh City, and on built-up land it is about the same as Bangkok’s. Jakarta’s gradient is the flattest on all land at both distances, and on built-up land over 30 km. In no column is Hanoi’s gradient clearly flatter than those of Bangkok and Jakarta. The statement of 2011 may have held for the city of 2009, for its built-up area of that time, or over a different range of distances; the data of this lesson cannot tell which.

Two general points also emerge. Dividing by built-up land gives a flatter gradient in every city, because the share of land that is built up itself falls with distance (Figure 4.2). And a single exponential describes Hanoi less well than Ho Chi Minh City (r² of against on all land): around Hoan Kiem Lake density falls steeply across the built-up core and then only slowly beyond it, a shape that one number cannot capture.

Interpretation — a network is not accessibilityKilometres of road say nothing about width, capacity or speed. In Ho Chi Minh City a large share of the network is narrow alleys, which carry motorcycles easily and cars hardly at all, so a kilometre of road there is not the same good as a kilometre of arterial road in Seoul. Lesson 3 measured what these differences produce — travel time — for one city; doing that for seven would need a network model for each of them.

4.5Exercises

Exercise 4.1Denser or more spread out?

Use Table 4.1 to compute the density of each urban centre in 1990 and compare it with the density of 2020. Which cities became denser, and which spread out faster than their population grew? Which of the conditions of Lesson 1 could explain each pattern?

Exercise 4.2Shape

On Map 4.1, use the circles to compare how far each urban centre of 2020 reaches from the centre of its frame. Which city is the most compact, and which the most elongated? Suggest one feature of geography or transport that could explain the shape of Ho Chi Minh City.

Exercise 4.3Two ways of growing

In Figure 4.2, read the edge of the continuously built-up core of each city in 1990, 2000 and 2015: the first ring in which less than half of the land is built up. Which core widened faster? Section 4.3 also shows that built-up land within 30 km grew faster around Hanoi. How can both statements be true? Use Map 4.2 to describe where the new built-up land of Hanoi appeared.

Exercise 4.4Gross and net density

Density on all land equals density on built-up land multiplied by the share of land that is built up. Taking logarithms, show that the gradient on all land equals the gradient on built-up land plus the rate at which the built-up share falls with distance. Then compare the two gradients over 30 km for each city in Table 4.2: where is the difference largest, and what does it tell you about the built-up share in that city?

Exercise 4.5Roads and journeys

Figure 4.4 compares networks, not journeys. Using Lesson 3, name two things that decide how long a trip takes but do not appear in this figure, and say which of them could be measured with the data of this course.

Exercise 4.6What the map cannot see

OpenStreetMap is drawn by volunteers. Give two reasons why its coverage of streets might be better in one of these cities than in another, and propose a check that would reveal such a difference.
