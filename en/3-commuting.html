Lesson 3

When commuting becomes cheaper

In Lesson 1 a fall in the cost of commuting spreads the city outwards and flattens its rent gradient. This lesson asks how the cost of reaching the centre of Ho Chi Minh City has changed: through the spread of the motorcycle, through new bridges, a tunnel and expressways, and through public transport. The effect of each new road link on travel times is measured with a model of the road network.

Data: the OpenStreetMap road network of September 2026; opening dates of major links checked word for word against government and press reports; figures on vehicles and public transport quoted from their sources. Travel times are produced by kich_ban/l3_thoi_gian_di_lai.py; the other numbers by kich_ban/l3_moc_ha_tang.mjs and kich_ban/l3_co_gioi.mjs.

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to:

  1. state what the commuting cost \(t\) of the model stands for in a real city, and name the changes that can lower or raise it;
  2. explain how a network model isolates the effect of individual road links on travel times, and state what such a model leaves out;
  3. read a travel-time map and compare the maps of different years;
  4. explain why the same link can change travel times by car and by motorcycle by very different amounts;
  5. relate the changes to the comparative statics of Lesson 1, and say why the relation is an interpretation rather than a test.

3.1Commuting cost in the model and in the city

In the model of Lesson 1, commuting costs \(t\) per kilometre of distance, so a household living \(x\) kilometres from the centre spends \(tx\) on commuting. The comparative statics of the model state what happens when \(t\) changes:

Figure 1.2 lets you lower \(t\) and watch the city spread: rent falls at the centre, rises at the edge, and the edge moves outwards. In a real city the cost of a trip has two parts, money and time, and both depend on the means of transport. The cost of reaching the centre can therefore fall for three reasons: a cheaper or faster means of transport spreads, new links shorten routes, or public transport reaches more places. It can also rise, when traffic grows faster than road space. The rest of this lesson takes these in turn for Ho Chi Minh City.

3.3New bridges, a tunnel and expressways

Between 2008 and 2026 the city and the national government opened the links listed in Table 3.2. Before the Thu Thiem Bridge, most people crossing from District 1 to Thu Thiem used a ferry: the operator reported about passengers a day before the bridge opened and about a day afterwards, and the ferry closed once the tunnel had opened.

To see what these links did to travel times, Map 3.1 uses a model of the road network. The network is the one mapped in OpenStreetMap in September 2026. For each earlier year, the links of Table 3.2 that had not yet opened are removed, and the Thu Thiem ferry is put back until 2012. The time from every road junction to Ben Thanh is then the shortest route over the network, at speeds assumed for each class of road. Motorcycles may not use expressways, and OpenStreetMap records some bridges, such as the Phu My Bridge, as closed to them; the model therefore finds a separate network for each mode.

How close are the modelled times to real ones? A report by VnExpress in 2026 published motorcycle travel times to Ben Thanh station for small hexagonal cells, also computed without congestion. 3.6Exercises

Exercise 3.1Cheaper commuting in the model

Use Figure 1.2 of Lesson 1. Move the slider for commuting cost to the left and describe what happens to rent at the centre, rent at the edge and the position of the edge. Which of these changes would you expect to see first in a city where only one corridor became cheaper, and why?

Exercise 3.2Motorcycles and cars

On Map 3.1, choose “Minutes saved” and switch between motorcycle and car. Name two places where the two modes gained very differently, and explain the difference with Table 3.2 and the note under Map 3.1.

Exercise 3.3What the network model leaves out

The model keeps every road other than those in Table 3.2 as it was in 2026, assumes constant speeds and ignores congestion. For each of these three assumptions, say whether it makes the gains of Figure 3.1 larger or smaller than the true gains, and why. Which assumption matters most for motorcycles?
