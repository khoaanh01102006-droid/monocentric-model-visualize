Lesson 1

The monocentric city model

Why housing costs more near the centre of a city, why dwellings there are smaller and buildings taller, and what determines how far the city extends.

The model is due to Alonso (1964), Muth (1969) and Mills (1967). Notation follows Brueckner (2011), chapter 2, “Analyzing Urban Spatial Structure”. Curves are computed in the browser from the model; numbers in the text are produced by kich_ban/l1_mo_hinh.mjs.

Learning objectives

After this lesson you should be able to:

  1. state the assumptions of the monocentric city model;
  2. derive the condition under which households are indifferent between locations, and explain why the price of housing falls with distance from the centre;
  3. explain why dwelling size rises, and building height and population density fall, with distance;
  4. determine the edge of the city, and predict how the city changes when population, income, commuting cost or agricultural rent changes.

1.1The pattern the model explains

Land and housing are more expensive near the centre of a city than at its edge. Buildings near the centre are taller, dwellings are smaller, and more people live on each square kilometre of land. The pattern appears in cities with very different histories, incomes and planning systems. The monocentric city model explains it with a small set of assumptions, and it remains the standard starting point for the analysis of urban spatial structure.

The lesson builds the model in three steps. Households choose where to live (Section 1.3). Developers decide how much floor space to build on each unit of land (Section 1.4). The land market then determines where the city ends (Section 1.5). Section 1.6 uses the completed model to compare cities that differ in population, income, commuting cost or agricultural rent.

1.2Assumptions

The version presented here has identical households and a fixed population, often called the closed-city model.

Closed and open city. In an open city households can move between cities at no cost, so their utility is fixed by the rest of the economy and the population adjusts. The two versions give different comparative statics (Brueckner, 1987).
  1. All jobs are located in a central business district (CBD), treated as a single point. A location is described by its distance \(x\), in kilometres, from the CBD.
  2. The city contains \(N\) identical households. Each has one worker, who earns income \(y\) in the CBD.
  3. Commuting costs \(t\) per kilometre of distance, so a household living at distance \(x\) spends \(tx\) on commuting.
  4. Households derive utility from floor space \(q\) and from a composite good \(c\) whose price is 1. The price of a square metre of floor space at distance \(x\) is \(p(x)\).
  5. Floor space is produced by developers, who combine land and capital. Land that is not used for housing is used in agriculture, where it earns rent \(r_A\).
  6. The population \(N\) is fixed. The level of utility that households reach is determined within the model.
Composite good. All consumption other than housing, measured in money.

To draw curves, the page uses Cobb–Douglas utility and production functions with the parameter values listed at the end of the lesson. The directions of the effects derived below do not depend on that choice.

1.3Households and the price of housing

A household living at distance \(x\) has \(y - tx\) left after paying for its commute. It divides this between floor space and the composite good:

\[ c + p(x)\,q = y - t\,x \]
(1.1)

Because households are identical, in equilibrium they must reach the same level of utility wherever they live. If households at one location were better off, others would move there and bid up the price of floor space until the advantage disappeared. This is the condition of spatial equilibrium.

Spatial equilibrium. No household can raise its utility by changing its location. With identical households, utility is the same at every occupied location.

Consider a household that moves one kilometre farther from the CBD. Its commuting cost rises by \(t\). Its utility can stay the same only if it saves the same amount on housing, which requires the price of each of its \(q\) square metres to fall by \(t/q\). For a small move this gives

\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial x} = -\,\frac{t}{q} \;<\; 0 \]
(1.2)

Equation (1.2) is usually called the Alonso–Muth condition. It states that the price of floor space falls with distance from the centre, and that it falls faster where dwellings are small. The function \(p(x)\) is the households’ bid rent for floor space.

Bid rent. The highest price per unit of floor space that a household would pay at a given location while reaching the equilibrium level of utility.

Floor space is cheaper farther out, so households there substitute towards space: \(q\) rises with \(x\). By equation (1.2), a larger \(q\) makes the price gradient flatter. The price curve is therefore steep near the centre and flatter towards the edge (Figure 1.1, step 1).

1.4Developers, building height and density

Developers produce floor space from land and capital. Let \(S\) denote capital per unit of land and \(h(S)\) the floor space produced on a unit of land, with \(h\) increasing and concave. A developer at distance \(x\) chooses \(S\) to maximise profit per unit of land, \(p(x)\,h(S) - iS - r(x)\), where \(i\) is the price of capital and \(r(x)\) the rent paid for land. The choice of \(S\) and competition among developers give

\[ p(x)\,h'(S) = i, \qquad r(x) = p(x)\,h(S) - i\,S \]
(1.3)

The first condition says that capital is added until the value of the extra floor space equals its cost. Where the price of floor space is high, this happens at a higher \(S\). Because \(p\) falls with distance, so do \(S\) and \(h\): the most floor space per unit of land is built at the centre. The second condition says that competition drives profit to zero, so land earns whatever is left after capital is paid.

Population density is floor space per unit of land divided by floor space per household:

\[ D(x) = \frac{h(x)}{q(x)} \]
(1.4)

Both terms work in the same direction. Moving outwards, \(h\) falls and \(q\) rises, so density falls. Land rent falls faster than the price of floor space, because both the price and the amount of floor space built on each unit of land decline.

1.5The edge of the city

Urban land use ends where housing can no longer outbid agriculture. At the edge of the city, \(\bar{x}\), land rent equals agricultural rent. In addition, all \(N\) households must be housed inside the edge:

\[ r(\bar{x}) = r_A \]
(1.5)
\[ \int_0^{\bar{x}} \theta\, x\, D(x)\, dx = N \]
(1.6)

Here \(\theta\) is the angle of land available for housing at each distance: \(2\pi\) for a complete circle, less where part of the land is water or is used for other purposes. Equations (1.5) and (1.6) together determine the edge of the city and the level of utility that households reach. With the parameters in Table 1.2, the edge lies km from the CBD.

Land available for housing. The computation assumes that of the land at each distance is residential, that is \(\theta\) equals that share of \(2\pi\). The value is illustrative.

Reading the results

Population. More households must be housed. The price of floor space rises at every distance, developers build more intensively, and the city extends outwards.

Income. Higher income raises the demand for floor space, so dwellings become larger. By equation (1.2), larger dwellings make the price gradient flatter. The price curve rotates: it falls near the centre, rises farther out, and the city expands.

Commuting cost. A higher \(t\) makes each kilometre more costly, so by equation (1.2) the price gradient becomes steeper. Prices rise near the centre and fall farther out, and the city contracts.

Agricultural rent. Land at the edge earns more in agriculture, so the city contracts. With a fixed population in a smaller area, the price of floor space rises at every distance.

Exercise 1.1The Alonso–Muth condition

Suppose that commuting costs VND 30,000 per kilometre of distance per month, and that a household living 10 km from the CBD occupies 50 m² of floor space.

  1. By how much must the monthly price per square metre fall between 10 km and 11 km for the household to be indifferent between the two locations?
  2. A household living 20 km from the CBD occupies 80 m². Is the price gradient steeper or flatter there? Give the value.
Show answer
(a) By equation (1.2), the price falls by t/q = 30,000 / 50 = VND 600 per m² per month. (b) Flatter: 30,000 / 80 = VND 375 per m² per month. Larger dwellings spread the extra commuting cost over more square metres.

Exercise 1.2Cheaper commuting

In Figure 1.2, reduce the commuting cost by 40 percent and leave the other conditions unchanged.

  1. What happens to the edge of the city?
  2. Does the price of housing at the centre rise or fall?
  3. Explain both results using equations (1.2), (1.5) and (1.6).
Show answer
(a) The edge moves outwards. (b) The price at the centre falls. (c) A lower t flattens the price gradient (1.2). Households can live farther out at lower cost, so the same population (1.6) spreads over a larger area; land rent now meets agricultural rent (1.5) at a greater distance, and competition for central locations weakens.
